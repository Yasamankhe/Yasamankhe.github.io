Robotic Piano Actuation System

I worked on the actuation hardware for a robotic piano system designed to physically press piano keys using custom solenoids. My work focused on the mechanical hand and actuator geometry, iterative CAD prototyping, solenoid integration, and a Python positioning model that calculated the motor positions required to reach all 54 keys.

01

Project Overview

The goal of the project was to build a system that could physically press piano keys using custom-built solenoids while moving the hand along the keyboard.

My work concentrated on the mechanical actuation subsystem. I developed the hand and joint geometry, worked with the custom solenoid design, iterated physical parts through CAD and FDM prototypes, and created the positioning model used to map piano keys to motor movement.

02

Solenoid Design

The solenoid had to fit within the dimensions of the hand base while still generating enough force to move the mechanical finger and press a piano key.

The housing and plunger were manufactured in the machine shop, and the coil was wound to fit inside the housing. During testing, the actuator operated at approximately 12 V and 600 mA, corresponding to roughly 20 Ω.

Housing
The housing used a 1.25-inch mild-steel square section with approximately 0.065-inch wall thickness to provide a low-reluctance magnetic path.
Plunger
The plunger used a 5/16-inch mild-steel rod so the magnetic path could extend through the moving actuator.
Guide Rod
The guide rod used 1/8-inch 304 stainless steel because it did not need to carry the magnetic flux.
Electrical Result
The completed actuator operated around 12 V, drew approximately 600 mA, and measured about 20 Ω, which aligned with the circuit team's electrical interface.
Why mild steel?

The magnetic housing was part of the flux path, so material permeability mattered. Mild steel provided a useful magnetic path while also behaving as a relatively magnetically soft material.

03

CAD & Mechanical Iteration

One of the main mechanical challenges was transferring enough solenoid motion and force to the piano key while keeping the assembly compact and aligned.

I designed the fingers and joints to reach both natural and sharp keys, which allowed the hand to move along the keyboard instead of being constrained to a single octave.

Air Gap
I iterated the geometry to reduce the solenoid air gap, improving the mechanical transfer from the moving plunger into the finger mechanism.
Joint Length
Multiple joints were printed in approximately 1 mm increments so the physical system could be adjusted quickly during integration.
Key Coverage
The finger geometry was designed around the different positions of natural and sharp piano keys rather than assuming a single uniform contact point.
Alignment
Fixed mounting points were incorporated into the parts to keep the actuator geometry aligned as the design was assembled and revised.
04

Key Positioning Model

Moving the hand approximately to a key was not enough. The actuator had to stop at a repeatable location so the finger could press the intended natural or sharp key.

I wrote a Python script that used the physical width of the keyboard, octave spacing, rack pitch, and gear tooth count to calculate a position for each of the 54 keys.

I also included an adjustable tick offset so the theoretical geometry could be calibrated against the assembled hardware once the rail and carriage positions were fixed.

Python
Piano Key Position Mapping
key_positioning.py 
import math


PITCH = 2.0
TEETH = 39

MM_REV = TEETH * PITCH

MILLIRAD_REV = 2.0 * math.pi * 1000.0

TICKS_PER_MM = MILLIRAD_REV / MM_REV


# Measured once the dovetails and rails are fixed
OFFSET_TICKS = 0


# Natural key width in mm
NATURAL = 22.25

OCTAVE = 7 * NATURAL


# Approximate center position of each note
# in units of natural-key width

CENTER = {
    'C':  0.5,
    'C#': 0.95,

    'D':  1.5,
    'D#': 2.05,

    'E':  2.5,

    'F':  3.5,
    'F#': 3.93,

    'G':  4.5,
    'G#': 5.0,

    'A':  5.5,
    'A#': 6.07,

    'B':  6.5
}


NOTES = [
    'C', 'C#',
    'D', 'D#',
    'E',
    'F', 'F#',
    'G', 'G#',
    'A', 'A#',
    'B'
]


NUM_KEYS = 54


keys = []


for i in range(NUM_KEYS):

    octave_index = i // 12

    note = NOTES[i % 12]

    mm = (
        octave_index * OCTAVE
        + CENTER[note] * NATURAL
    )

    keys.append(mm)


for i, mm in enumerate(keys):

    ticks = round(
        mm * TICKS_PER_MM
        + OFFSET_TICKS
    )

    comma = "," if i < NUM_KEYS - 1 else ""

    print(
        f"    {ticks:<8}{comma} [{i}]"
    )
Why include an offset?

The calculated positions describe the ideal geometry, but the real carriage and rail assembly introduces mounting tolerances. The offset lets the software be calibrated to the physical machine without changing the geometry model for every key.

05

System Integration

The hand was only one part of the complete robotic piano system, so the mechanical geometry, solenoid electrical requirements, motor positioning, and control electronics all had to agree with each other.

I designed my part of the system around those interfaces rather than treating the mechanical structure in isolation. The measured solenoid electrical behavior matched the requirements of the team's circuit hardware, while the Python model provided the movement positions needed by the motor-control side.

Mechanical

Physical Fit

The actuator, finger, joints, rail carriage, and piano geometry had to fit within the same constrained mechanical system.

Electrical

Solenoid Interface

The actuator's measured voltage, current, and resistance were compatible with the drive electronics developed by the circuit team.

Software

Motor Position

The Python model converted the mechanical geometry into motor positions that could be consumed by the motion-control implementation.

Result

54-Key Coverage

The completed system was designed to move across and actuate all 54 keys rather than remaining fixed over one small section of the keyboard.
