Robotic Piano Actuation System

This was a course group project. My contribution centered on the electromechanical actuation and physical integration of the robotic hand. I machined solenoid components, wound and tested the electromagnetic coils, iterated the finger linkage geometry, soldered and managed actuator wiring, integrated the moving hand assembly, and developed the Python positioning model used to calculate carriage positions across all 54 piano keys.

02

Electromagnetic Solenoid Design

The solenoid was not treated as a simple off-the-shelf actuator. The geometry, magnetic materials, coil winding, available 12 V supply, current draw, plunger travel, and mechanical linkage all influenced whether the finger could produce enough force to press a key.

One of the most important constraints was the air gap in the magnetic path. Air has much lower permeability than the steel components, so even a relatively small air gap contributes strongly to magnetic reluctance. Reducing that gap through mechanical iteration was therefore an important way to improve actuator force without relying only on additional current.

Magnetic Housing
I used mild steel for the housing so it could form part of the low-reluctance magnetic path around the coil and plunger.
Plunger
The moving plunger also used mild steel so the magnetic flux path extended through the moving actuator.
Guide Rod
The guide rod used 304 stainless steel because it served a mechanical guidance function rather than acting as a major part of the magnetic flux path.
Coil Wire
I wound the actuator using 30 AWG insulated magnet wire and measured resistance during winding rather than waiting until the coil was finished.
Electrical Target
The actuator was designed around a 12 V supply. A 20 Ω coil draws about 0.6 A, while a 15 Ω coil draws about 0.8 A, so coil resistance directly constrained the operating current.
Force Optimization
I coupled the electromagnetic and mechanical design by adjusting the linkage and actuator position to reduce unnecessary air gap and improve the transfer of plunger motion into the finger.
Coil resistance was a design parameter, not just a measurement

With a fixed 12 V drive, coil resistance determines current through Ohm's law. More current can increase magnetic field strength, but it also increases winding heating. I therefore checked resistance during winding while developing the actuator instead of treating the coil as a purely mechanical component.

04

Finger & Linkage Iteration

The actuator could generate force, but that force still had to reach the piano key through the printed finger mechanism. Linkage geometry therefore became part of the actuator design rather than a separate mechanical detail.

I printed multiple revisions with different lengths, mounting locations, and joint geometry. Some dimensions were changed in approximately 1 mm increments so I could compare physical behavior without redesigning the entire mechanism between tests.

Air Gap

Reduce Magnetic Separation

I adjusted the geometry to let the solenoid operate with a smaller effective gap where possible, improving magnetic actuation.

Leverage

Mechanical Advantage

Link length and pivot location affected the relationship between actuator travel and force delivered at the piano key.

Alignment

Repeatable Contact

The linkage had to contact natural and sharp keys consistently while the complete hand moved along the keyboard.

Assembly

Fastened Interface

The solenoid interface and finger linkage were designed to fit and screw together before being mounted onto the hand base.

05

Wiring, Soldering & System Integration

Once the individual actuators worked, the harder problem became integrating them into a moving system. The carriage contained mechanical joints, solenoids, wiring, drive electronics, structural parts, and rail interfaces in a limited amount of space.

I worked on actuator wire routing, harness organization, soldering, and mechanical integration. Wiring had to remain clear of the finger joints and moving hardware while still allowing the carriage to travel along the keyboard.

Integration was iterative. Whenever the geometry or actuator position changed, I often had to re-check wire routing, soldered connections, clearances, and mechanical movement before testing the system again.

Harness Routing
Actuator wires were organized around the moving assembly so they did not interfere with finger motion, rails, or carriage movement.
Soldering
I soldered actuator wiring and reworked connections as components and mechanical layouts changed during integration.
Mechanical Clearance
The harness and electrical connections had to fit around the linkage while preserving the motion needed for physical key actuation.
Final Integration
Solenoids, finger mechanisms, wiring, carriage hardware, electronics, and positioning all had to operate together as one system.
Integration was one of the largest parts of the project

A working solenoid or a working finger by itself was not enough. The system only worked when the actuator, linkage, wiring, electronics, carriage, and positioning were compatible with each other. A large portion of my lab work was spent finding and fixing those interfaces.

06

Key Positioning Model

The moving hand had to stop at repeatable locations across the keyboard so the correct finger could physically align with the intended key.

I wrote a Python script that used the keyboard geometry, rack pitch, gear tooth count, and a calibratable hardware offset to generate motor positions for all 54 keys.

Python
Piano Key Position Mapping
key_positioning.py 
import math


PITCH = 2.0
TEETH = 39

MM_REV = TEETH * PITCH

MILLIRAD_REV = 2.0 * math.pi * 1000.0

TICKS_PER_MM = MILLIRAD_REV / MM_REV


# Calibrated against the assembled carriage
OFFSET_TICKS = 0


NATURAL = 22.25

OCTAVE = 7 * NATURAL


CENTER = {
    'C':  0.5,
    'C#': 0.95,

    'D':  1.5,
    'D#': 2.05,

    'E':  2.5,

    'F':  3.5,
    'F#': 3.93,

    'G':  4.5,
    'G#': 5.0,

    'A':  5.5,
    'A#': 6.07,

    'B':  6.5
}


NOTES = [
    'C', 'C#',
    'D', 'D#',
    'E',
    'F', 'F#',
    'G', 'G#',
    'A', 'A#',
    'B'
]


NUM_KEYS = 54


keys = []


for i in range(NUM_KEYS):

    octave_index = i // 12

    note = NOTES[i % 12]

    mm = (
        octave_index * OCTAVE
        + CENTER[note] * NATURAL
    )

    keys.append(mm)


for i, mm in enumerate(keys):

    ticks = round(
        mm * TICKS_PER_MM
        + OFFSET_TICKS
    )

    comma = "," if i < NUM_KEYS - 1 else ""

    print(
        f"    {ticks:<8}{comma} [{i}]"
    )
Geometry plus calibration

The mathematical model provided the relative key positions, while the adjustable offset allowed the calculated positions to be aligned with the physical rail and carriage after assembly.
