01

System Overview

The controller was designed as one node within the larger rover electrical architecture. It receives power directly from the rover electrical system, generates the required lower-voltage rails, communicates over CAN, and controls the lighting output through an STM32 microcontroller.

The project covered the full hardware lifecycle: requirements, circuit design, component selection, PCB layout, fabrication, hand assembly, firmware, staged bring-up, communication testing, PWM validation, and final system integration.

Power

21 to 29.4 V rover input with regulated internal rails for the embedded electronics.

Processing

STM32 microcontroller handles network commands, firmware logic, PWM output, and board control.

Communication

CAN provides communication between the lighting controller and the rest of the rover network.

Lighting

Dedicated constant-current LED circuitry provides controlled drive while PWM changes output behavior.

Protection

External power and communication interfaces include protection against electrical transients.

Development Interfaces

SWD, UART, USB, and CAN interfaces support programming, communication, debugging, and hardware validation.

02

Engineering Decisions

The board architecture was driven by the requirements of operating as part of a mobile rover rather than as a standalone laboratory circuit.

Why 21 to 29.4 V input?
The lighting controller had to operate directly from the rover's existing battery and power-distribution architecture. Designing for the complete battery voltage range avoided requiring a separate external supply for the subsystem.
Why CAN?
CAN was the communication interface used by the rover electrical network. Using CAN allowed the lighting board to operate as a distributed controller and receive commands from the larger rover system rather than requiring dedicated point-to-point control wiring.
Why 4 layers?
The board combines switching power conversion, microcontroller circuitry, communication interfaces, and multiple power rails in a compact layout. A four-layer stack provided a continuous internal ground reference, dedicated internal power distribution, and more controlled routing than a two-layer implementation.
Why STM32?
The microcontroller provides the digital peripherals needed for CAN communication, PWM generation, firmware control, programming, and debugging while fitting naturally into the rover's embedded electronics architecture.
Why constant-current LEDs?
The LED load is better controlled by regulating current rather than relying only on a fixed voltage. The dedicated driver provides predictable LED current while allowing the microcontroller to adjust output through PWM.
Why PWM?
PWM provided software-controlled adjustment of illumination without changing the underlying constant-current drive. This allowed lighting behavior to be changed dynamically for rover tasks, including obstacle approach and Morse-code-related operation.
03

Circuit Design

The schematic combines the functions required for a complete embedded subsystem rather than only the LED load. The design includes the input power stage, voltage conversion, embedded controller, CAN physical layer, lighting driver, programming interfaces, connectors, and protection.

Power Conversion
A buck converter generates the 5 V rail from the rover battery input, with additional regulation providing the 3.3 V electronics rail.
STM32
The microcontroller section includes decoupling, clocking, reset, programming, communication, and PWM connections required for embedded operation.
CAN Interface
A dedicated CAN transceiver converts the STM32 logic-level signals to the differential physical layer used by the rover CAN bus.
Lighting Driver
Constant-current LED driving controls the physical lighting load while PWM from the microcontroller adjusts operating behavior.
Protection
Power and CAN interfaces include transient and ESD protection so external electrical events are not passed directly into sensitive circuitry.
04

PCB Layout

Images 01 through 04 show the transition from the mechanical PCB representation to the actual copper implementation. The layout was organized around the very different electrical requirements of the switching power stage, microcontroller, CAN interface, and LED circuitry.

Ground Reference
An internal layer provides a continuous ground reference beneath the signal layers, reducing dependence on fragmented ground routing.
Power Distribution
An internal power layer and copper pours distribute the regulated supply across the board while keeping higher-current paths appropriately sized.
Switching Region
The buck converter, inductor, input/output capacitors, and switching-node routing were kept compact to reduce the physical area of the high-current switching loop.
Digital Region
STM32 control, communication, programming, and low-voltage circuitry were kept away from the highest-noise switching region where possible.
05

Assembly & Bring-Up

Five copies of the board were assembled and hand soldered. Bring-up was performed in stages so that power integrity, programming, communication, and load control could be verified independently before full-system operation.

1. Inspection
Checked component orientation, connector placement, polarity, solder joints, and possible bridges before applying power.
2. Power Rails
Verified the expected regulated rails before programming the microcontroller or attaching the board to the complete rover system.
3. MCU Programming
Programmed the STM32 through the debug interface and confirmed basic firmware execution.
4. Communication
Used a USB-CAN adapter to send commands and verify communication between the host and the controller.
5. Lighting Output
Exercised the LED driver and varied the lighting output through PWM commands.
6. Rover Integration
Connected the validated controller to the rover electrical system and confirmed operation in the final application.

07

Rover Integration

The final image in the progression shows the subsystem operating after installation on the rover. This step tested more than whether the board could simply power on. The controller had to operate from the rover electrical supply, communicate within the system, and control the physical lighting load in the intended environment.

PWM control allowed the lighting output to be changed dynamically. This was useful during rover tasks involving Morse-code-related operation and adjustment of illumination as the rover approached obstacles used during puzzle-solving activities.

Board-level validation to system-level validation

Successful rover operation closed the loop between schematic design, PCB implementation, firmware, communication, power delivery, lighting control, and the final mechanical and electrical integration.
