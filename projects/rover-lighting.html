01

System Overview

The lighting controller was developed as an embedded subsystem for the rover electrical architecture. It receives power from the rover battery, generates the required regulated voltage rails, communicates with the rest of the rover over CAN, and controls the lighting output through an STM32 microcontroller.

The project covered the full development cycle, beginning with circuit design and PCB layout and continuing through fabrication, hand assembly, firmware development, bench testing, communication validation, and final rover integration.

Power Input

Designed around the rover's 21 to 29.4 V battery range and converted to the internal voltage rails required by the electronics.

STM32 Control

The microcontroller receives commands and manages communication, PWM behavior, and lighting control.

CAN Interface

CAN communication allows the board to operate as a node within the wider rover electrical network.

LED Driver

Constant-current driving provides controlled LED current while PWM allows adjustable lighting behavior.

Protection

Power and communication interfaces include transient protection appropriate for the rover electrical environment.

Programming & Debug

SWD and communication interfaces support firmware programming, hardware bring-up, testing, and debugging.

02

Circuit Design

The schematic linked above represents the complete electrical implementation of the controller. Rather than treating the lighting as an isolated load, the board combines the supporting power, processing, communication, and protection circuitry needed for operation as a rover subsystem.

Power Conversion
The rover battery input is converted to lower regulated rails used by the microcontroller, communication circuitry, and supporting electronics.
Microcontroller
STM32 circuitry includes its required power, clocking, reset, programming, and interface connections.
CAN
A CAN transceiver interfaces the microcontroller with the rover communication bus and provides the physical layer required for network communication.
Lighting
Dedicated constant-current LED circuitry controls the lighting load while the STM32 supplies PWM commands for dynamic brightness and signaling behavior.
Protection
Protection components were included around the external power and communication interfaces to improve robustness against electrical transients.
03

PCB Design

Images 01 through 04 show the transition from the mechanical board representation to the completed physical layout. The controller was implemented as a four-layer PCB with the component side carrying the power, control, CAN, programming, and interface circuitry while the opposite side carries the lighting hardware.

The routing and polygon images show the actual copper implementation beneath the 3D render. Layout decisions focused on separating the switching power region from the microcontroller circuitry, keeping important current paths compact, providing a continuous ground reference, and routing communication and control signals through the board.

04

Assembly & Bring-Up

Image 05 shows the fabricated design after physical assembly. Five boards were assembled and hand soldered, moving the project from a PCB layout into functional hardware.

Bring-up was performed progressively rather than immediately exercising the full system. The boards were inspected, power rails were checked, the STM32 was programmed, and communication and lighting functions were tested before integration onto the rover.

Inspection
Checked assembly quality, component orientation, connectors, polarity, and potential solder faults.
Power-Up
Verified the expected voltage rails before moving to digital programming and functional testing.
Firmware
Programmed the STM32 and exercised the controller through its embedded firmware.
Communication
Used USB-CAN communication to send commands and verify feedback from the board.
05

Rover Integration

Image 06 shows the lighting system operating after installation on the rover. This was the final step in the progression shown above, moving from an isolated controller on the bench to a subsystem working as part of the complete rover.

PWM control allowed the lighting output to change dynamically. This supported rover tasks involving Morse code signaling and changes in illumination during obstacle approach and puzzle-solving activities.

System-level validation

The rover test demonstrates that the controller was not only fabricated and powered independently, but also integrated with the communication, power, and task requirements of the larger system.

06

Validation Process

The validation video shown directly beneath the progression gallery captures part of the bench-testing process. A USB-CAN adapter was used to communicate with the controller, issue commands, and observe feedback from the hardware.

PWM commands were then used to change the lighting output, confirming that communication, embedded firmware, microcontroller control, and the lighting hardware were operating together before installation on the rover.

CAN Test
Commands were transmitted to the controller over the CAN interface using the USB-CAN bench setup.
Feedback
Returned status and command feedback provided confirmation that communication with the board was functioning.
PWM Test
Lighting intensity was varied through PWM commands to confirm end-to-end control of the physical output.
