Rover Lighting Control System

I designed this board as the lighting controller for our rover. I wanted one board that could take power directly from the rover, communicate over CAN, control the LEDs from an STM32, and give me the programming and debugging interfaces I needed during bring-up. I designed the schematic and PCB, assembled the boards, wrote and tested the firmware, and then integrated the finished system onto the rover.

01

How I Designed It

I designed the board to work as one node on the rover's electrical system. I take the rover battery input, convert it to the lower voltages I need, receive commands over CAN, and use the STM32 to control the LED driver.

I also wanted the board to be easy to bring up and debug, so I included SWD, UART, USB, and CAN access instead of depending on only one interface.

Power

I designed the input for the rover's 21 to 29.4 V battery range and generated the lower voltage rails on the board.

STM32

I used the STM32 to handle commands, PWM generation, communication, and the lighting behavior.

CAN

I connected the board to the rover's CAN network so it could receive commands from the rest of the system.

LED Driver

I used a constant-current driver for the LEDs and controlled the output with PWM from the STM32.

Protection

I added protection around the external power and CAN connections because those are the parts of the board exposed to the rover wiring.

Debugging

I included SWD, UART, USB, and CAN access so I could program the MCU and test different parts of the board during bring-up.

02

Design Decisions

A lot of the design came from the fact that this board had to work inside the rover, not just on my bench.

Why 21 to 29.4 V?
I designed around that range because that is the battery voltage the board sees on the rover. I wanted the controller to connect directly to the rover power system instead of needing another external supply.
Why CAN?
I used CAN because the rover already uses CAN between its electrical subsystems. That let me make the lighting controller another node on the same network instead of adding separate control wiring.
Why 4 layers?
I chose four layers because I had switching power, the STM32, several voltage rails, CAN, USB, and the LED circuitry on a fairly compact board. I wanted a solid ground plane and a cleaner way to distribute power without breaking up my return paths.
Why STM32?
I used an STM32 because I needed CAN, PWM, firmware control, and normal embedded debugging and programming support on the same MCU.
Why constant current?
I wanted the LED current to be controlled directly instead of depending only on a voltage and resistor. I used a dedicated LED driver and then used PWM when I wanted to change the lighting level.
Why PWM?
I wanted the software to be able to change the light output without changing the hardware. PWM also became useful for the rover tasks where I needed different lighting behavior for Morse code and obstacle approach.
03

PCB Layout

I separated the switching power area from the STM32 and communication circuitry as much as the board size allowed. I kept the switching components close together because I wanted the high-current switching loop to stay small.

I used one internal layer as a continuous ground plane and another internal layer for power distribution. That made the routing much cleaner and gave the signals a better ground reference than I would have had on a two-layer version.

Ground Plane
I kept an internal ground layer as continuous as possible so I did not have to route ground around the board with long traces.
Power
I used the internal power layer and copper pours to distribute the rails and give the higher-current paths more copper.
Buck Converter
I kept the converter, inductor, and nearby capacitors close together and kept the switching node compact.
STM32 Area
I kept the microcontroller and its low-voltage circuitry away from the main switching area where I could.
04

Assembly & Bring-Up

I assembled and hand soldered five boards. Before I powered them, I checked the component orientation, connector placement, polarity, and solder joints.

This was my first PCB revision, and I did not need to respin the board. Once I assembled it, I was able to power it, program the STM32, communicate with it, control the LEDs, and eventually run the same hardware on the rover.

1. Inspection
I checked for solder bridges, wrong orientation, polarity mistakes, and assembly problems before applying power.
2. Power Rails
I powered the board in stages and checked the regulated rails before moving on to the MCU.
3. Programming
I programmed the STM32 through SWD and confirmed that my firmware was running.
4. CAN
I connected a USB-CAN adapter and sent commands to the board while checking the returned feedback.
5. PWM
I changed the PWM command and checked that the physical lighting output changed with it.
6. Rover
After the bench tests, I connected the board to the rover power and CAN system and tested it in the actual application.

06

Rover Integration

Once I was happy with the bench tests, I installed the lighting system on the rover. At that point I was testing more than the PCB by itself. I was checking whether it worked with the rover battery, CAN network, wiring, firmware, and the actual lighting load together.

I used PWM to change the lighting dynamically. We used that behavior for tasks involving Morse code and for changing the lighting as the rover moved closer to an obstacle during puzzle-solving tasks.

What the rover photo shows

The photo is not my only proof that the electronics worked. I use it mainly to show that the same board I designed and tested on the bench was eventually installed and operating on the actual rover.
