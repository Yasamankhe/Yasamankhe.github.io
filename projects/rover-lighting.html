Rover Lighting Control System

I designed this board as the lighting controller for our rover. I wanted one board that could take power directly from the rover, communicate over CAN, control the LEDs from an STM32, and give me enough access for programming and debugging during bring-up. I designed the schematic and PCB, assembled the boards, worked on the lighting firmware and CAN messages, tested the hardware, and integrated the finished system onto the rover.

4-layer PCB
21 to 29.4 V input
5 boards assembled
~0.75 A at 100% PWM
STM32 control
CAN communication

01

How I Designed It

I designed the controller to work as one node on the rover's electrical system. The board takes the rover battery input, generates the lower voltage rails I need, receives commands over CAN, and uses the STM32 to control the lighting.

I also wanted enough access during development that one broken interface would not leave me stuck, so I included SWD, UART, USB, and CAN on the board.

Power

I designed the board for the final 21 to 29.4 V rover battery range and generated 5 V and 3.3 V rails locally.

STM32

I used the STM32F103 to handle CAN commands, PWM generation, feedback, heartbeat messages, and the lighting behavior.

CAN

I connected the board to the rover CAN network through a TCAN334G transceiver.

LED Driver

I used the KTD2505R to drive the LEDs with controlled current and used its control input for PWM dimming.

Protection

I added input fuse protection, a TVS on the rover power input, and dedicated protection on CANH and CANL.

Development

I programmed the board through SWD with an ST-Link and also included UART, USB, and CAN for testing and debugging.

02

Power & Protection

I wanted the lighting board to connect directly to the rover power distribution rather than needing another external supply. I use an LMR51420 buck converter for the 5 V rail and a TPS7A2033 regulator for 3.3 V.

At 100% PWM I measured about 0.75 A input current on one lighting board. I used a 1 A input fuse so normal full-output operation stays below the fuse rating while still giving the board protection against a larger fault current.

Input Fuse
I used a 1 A fuse at the board input. The part reference shown in the schematic was originally there because I already had the correct PCB footprint, but the assembled boards use the 1 A fuse I selected for the final hardware.
Input TVS
The board was first being designed around a lower rover voltage. When the rover changed to the 24 V nominal system, I replaced the original input TVS with a higher-voltage-rated part that fit the same footprint.
Reverse Polarity
I considered adding a P-channel MOSFET for reverse-polarity protection. I decided not to add that stage because the XT30 connector is mechanically keyed and cannot normally be connected backwards.
5 V Rail
I use the LMR51420 switching regulator to step the rover input down to 5 V for the lighting and supporting circuitry.
3.3 V Rail
I use the TPS7A2033 to generate the 3.3 V rail for the STM32 and the low-voltage electronics.
Requirement change during the project

The change from the earlier lower-voltage assumption to the final 24 V rover system happened after the PCB was already designed. Because the replacement TVS used the same footprint, I could update the protection device without changing the PCB layout.

03

CAN Interface

I used CAN because the rover already communicates between its electrical subsystems over CAN. That meant I could make each lighting board another node on the existing network instead of adding separate control wiring.

I use a TCAN334G transceiver between the STM32 and the physical CAN bus. Since CANH and CANL leave the PCB and connect into the rover harness, I added an ESD2CAN24 protection device directly on the bus lines.

Transceiver
I used the TCAN334G to translate between the STM32 logic signals and the differential CANH and CANL bus.
CAN Protection
I placed an ESD2CAN24 protection device on CANH and CANL so external ESD and transient events on the rover wiring are not passed directly into the transceiver.
Split Termination
I included two 60 Ω resistors with a 4.7 nF capacitor at the midpoint. The two resistors form the 120 Ω CAN termination, and the split midpoint capacitor helps with high-frequency common-mode noise.
Five Boards
I wanted one PCB design that I could use in all five lighting positions, so I included the termination network on every PCB. I only populated the termination on the boards at the two physical ends of the CAN bus. The interior boards do not have the termination populated.
04

LED Control

I did not want the LED current to depend only on a supply voltage and resistor. I used a KTD2505R constant-current LED driver and controlled it from the STM32.

The LED stage uses the driver together with its inductor, rectifier, local capacitors, and feedback resistor. I use the CTRL input for PWM so I can change the light output in software without changing the hardware current-setting network.

Driver
KTD2505R constant-current LED driver.
Inductor
I used a 10 µH inductor in the LED driver switching stage.
PWM
The STM32 drives the CTRL input so I can change the brightness and lighting behavior from firmware.
05

Firmware & CAN Software

I configured the STM32 peripherals in STM32CubeMX and developed and flashed the firmware from STM32CubeIDE. I programmed and debugged the physical boards through SWD using an ST-Link.

The team already had a shared CAN communication library being ported from another project. That library handled the common packing and unpacking structure, used the CAN frame ID for message type information, and kept the normal CAN payload to eight bytes. I integrated that library into the lighting firmware and added the lighting-specific messages and behavior I needed.

PWM Commands
I added the lighting-side handling for incoming PWM commands and applied the requested value to the physical LED output.
Feedback
After applying a command, I added feedback so the board could report the resulting PWM value back over CAN instead of only receiving commands.
Heartbeat
I added periodic heartbeat messages so I could tell from the CAN bus that each lighting board was still alive even when I was not sending it a new PWM command.
Simultaneous RX / TX
I specifically tested receiving PWM commands while the STM32 was also transmitting heartbeat and feedback messages. I had some issues while getting this working, but I was able to get simultaneous receive and transmit working correctly.
Five Boards
I flashed all five lighting boards with my STM32 firmware before the full rover integration.

06

Python CAN Testing

For the bench setup I wrote a Python test script using python-can. I connected the laptop to the CAN bus through a USB-CAN adapter and used the gs_usb interface.

I had to configure the USB adapter with Zadig so Windows would expose it through the correct driver. Once that was working, I used the Python script and can.logger to send commands and watch the traffic coming back from the board.

python-can
I used python-can as the interface between my test script and the CAN adapter.
gs_usb
I used the gs_usb backend for the USB-CAN adapter connection.
Zadig
I configured the adapter's Windows USB driver with Zadig before python-can could communicate with it correctly.
can.logger
I used can.logger while testing so I could watch commands, feedback frames, and heartbeat messages on the bus.
What I Checked
From my laptop I could send a new PWM value, see the physical lighting change, receive the board's feedback, and continue seeing heartbeat messages showing that the STM32 was active.
07

PCB Layout

When I laid out the board, I tried to think about where the current actually flows instead of only whether I could physically route every connection.

I used four layers because the board combines switching power, an STM32, CAN, USB, several voltage rails, and the LED driver in a fairly small area. I kept one internal layer as a solid ground plane and used the other internal layer for power distribution.

Decoupling
I placed the small ceramic decoupling capacitors close to the IC supply pins. I also used larger local capacitors so the board had both high-frequency decoupling and local energy storage.
Ground Plane
I kept one internal layer as a solid ground reference so signals had a short return path instead of depending on long ground traces.
VIN / 5 V / 3.3 V
I used wider copper and polygon pours for important power nets including VIN, 5 V, and 3.3 V instead of treating them like normal low-current signal traces.
LMR51420
I kept the buck converter, L2, and the nearby input and output capacitors close together so the high-current switching loop stayed compact.
KTD2505R
I followed the same idea around the LED driver by keeping U11, the 10 µH inductor, rectifier, and local capacitors close to the switching stage.
Noise Separation
I tried to keep the noisier switching areas away from the STM32, crystal, CAN, and low-voltage control circuitry where the board geometry allowed it.
08

Assembly & Bring-Up

I assembled and hand soldered five boards. Before I applied power I checked component orientation, polarity, connector placement, and solder joints.

This was my first PCB revision and I did not need a board respin. After assembly I was able to bring up the power rails, program the STM32, get CAN communication working, control the lighting, and later use the same hardware on the rover.

1. Inspection
I checked for solder bridges, wrong component orientation, polarity mistakes, and obvious assembly problems before powering the board.
2. Power
I powered the board and checked the regulated rails before moving on to the microcontroller.
3. SWD
I connected an ST-Link through the SWD header and programmed the STM32 from STM32CubeIDE.
4. CAN
I connected the USB-CAN adapter and worked through the CAN communication until I could reliably send commands and receive feedback.
5. PWM
I tested different PWM values and checked that the physical lighting changed as expected.
6. All Five Boards
I flashed the firmware onto all five boards before the final rover integration.

10

Rover Integration

Once I was happy with the bench tests, I installed the lighting system on the rover. At that point I was no longer testing only the PCB. I was checking whether the boards worked with the rover battery, CAN network, wiring, firmware, and actual lighting load together.

I used PWM to change the lighting dynamically. We used that behavior for tasks involving Morse code and for adjusting the lighting as the rover moved closer to an obstacle during puzzle-solving tasks.

Why I included the rover photo

The photo is not the only evidence that the board worked. I include it because it shows that the same hardware I designed, assembled, programmed, and tested on the bench was eventually installed and operating on the rover.
